It isn't reading what you know.
It's reading how you turn.

Topics are the easy part, and the part you can look up. What makes a sentence yours is smaller and much harder to fake: where you breathe, what you refuse to say, the word you reach for eleven times a year. Here is the sort of thing it comes back with after a first pass through an archive.

rhythm

Sentences from six to forty-one words. You like the long one immediately after three short ones.

openings

One paragraph in five begins with And, But or So. You almost never open with a question you intend to answer.

diction

Actually, eleven times a year. Moreover, never. You say bought where most people would say invested in.

marks

No semicolons in four years. Em dashes everywhere, usually carrying the second thought rather than an aside.

endings

You finish a section on its shortest sentence, and you never explain the joke you just made.

refusals

You don't use journey, unlock, leverage, or any sentence that begins In a world where.

How it learns you.

  1. Hand over the archive.

    Newsletters, drafts, the book you stopped writing in 2023, the long replies you send at midnight. Messy is fine; messy is the point. It reads all of it and keeps none of it anywhere another person can reach.

  2. It learns the habits, not the facts.

    Voice and knowledge are tuned apart on purpose. The model learns how you move through a sentence; anything it needs to know stays in your files, where it can be quoted and checked rather than remembered and guessed at.

  3. You write with it, and it keeps learning from the cuts.

    It drafts, you cut. What you cut is the most useful thing you own — a running record of what you would never have said — and it goes straight back into the next tuning. The longer you use it, the less there is to cut.
