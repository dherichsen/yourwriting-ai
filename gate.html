THE HALLUCINATION GATE

How the gate decides.

A tuned model writes in your voice, which is exactly what makes a wrong figure inside it dangerous. The gate is the part that refuses to let one through — five checks on every draft, before a person sees it.

The whole gate, in one picture.

Five checks and one verdict on every claim. The middle step is the one that decides — and it decides the way a person would: with an answer and how sure it is of it.

  1. STEP 1SplitClaims, not sentences — figures, dates, names, commitments.
  2. STEP 2RetrieveOnly the files you named. No web, no memory of its own.
  3. STEP 3 · DECIDESCheckA typed verdict + how sure it is.
  4. STEP 4RepairSwap in the real line, or turn it into a question.
  5. STEP 5SignA receipt travels with the draft, claim by claim.
Step 3 · what the check returns

Not a paragraph — a typed verdict (supported · repaired · held) with a calibrated confidence. A verdict is a fixed choice, so the gate can't write a fluent wrong one, and the confidence is honest: what it calls 90% sure is right about 90% of the time. Above the bar it clears the claim itself; below it, a person decides. Nothing is published on a guess.

SUPPORTED — a line in your files entails it REPAIRED — swapped for the real value HELD — unsourced or not sure enough
Confident & sourced → clears itselfThe claim goes out, with its line on the receipt. No one is asked to re-read what the gate already settled.
Wrong, unsourced, or unsure → held for a personIt never keeps its guess. A reviewer settles four flagged lines instead of re-reading the whole letter.
  1. 1 · Split

    The draft is broken into claims, not sentences.

    A sentence can carry three claims and one opinion. The gate separates them: figures, dates, names, commitments and quotes each become a claim of their own, with the span of text it lives in. Opinions and pleasantries are left alone — there is nothing to check in “happy to help”.

  2. 2 · Retrieve

    Each claim is looked up in the sources you named.

    The gate searches only what you pointed it at: the invoice folder, the contract store, the pricing sheet, the policy library, the ticket history. It is not a web search and it has no memory of its own — if the claim is not in your files, there is nowhere else for it to come from.

  3. 3 · Check

    Supported, or not in your files. There is no third answer.

    A claim passes when a line in a named file entails it. Close is not good enough: “about ten days” fails against a table whose last four rows are 18, 21, 19 and 22 days. Stale sources fail too — a figure from a superseded contract is flagged with the clause that replaced it.

  4. 4 · Repair

    It takes the real line, or it asks.

    A failed claim has two honest endings. Either the file contains the right value and the gate substitutes it, marked so the writer can see what changed, or nothing in your files settles it and the sentence becomes a question: “I'll confirm the date and come back to you today.” The model never gets to keep its guess.

  5. 5 · Sign

    The receipt travels with the draft.

    Every claim, the file and line it came from, the ones that were repaired, and the ones a person still has to settle. Your reviewer reads four lines instead of re-reading the letter, and your log keeps the same record for the auditor who asks in eighteen months.

What a receipt looks like.

It is attached to the draft in your queue and written to the log. Nothing here leaves your building.
