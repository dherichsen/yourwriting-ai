The gate

What exactly is a “hallucination gate”?

A check that runs between the model and the person. The draft is split into claims, every claim is looked up in the files you named as sources, and the draft only goes forward when each one is supported — repaired from the file, or turned into a question when nothing settles it. A receipt listing each claim and its source line travels with the draft. Full detail on the gate page.

Doesn't the model already cite its sources?

Models are good at producing citations and bad at guaranteeing them; a fluent citation to a document that does not say that thing is the most common failure we see. The gate does not ask the model to be honest, it checks the claim against the file itself and marks the answer.

What happens when a claim can't be checked?

It is never quietly dropped. Either the file contains the right value and the gate substitutes it (marked, so the writer sees what changed), or the sentence becomes an explicit commitment to confirm. Your reviewer sees both cases in the receipt.

Can we see what it caught?

Every checked claim writes a line to a local log: the claim, the source it matched, the verdict, the repair. Your security and quality people can read it; we cannot.

Fine-tuning

Why tune instead of just prompting?

Prompts are a costume over a rented model: they drift, they cost tokens on every call, and they do not survive a long queue. A thin adapter — about 0.4% of the weights — learns your formats and vocabulary once and holds on the ten thousandth draft.

How many examples do you need?

Six hundred to two thousand pieces of writing you would sign, for one kind of letter. More matters less than consistency: two thousand drafts by six different people with six different habits teach the model to be inconsistent.

How long does a tune take?

Four weeks for one kind of writing: a week to gather, a week to grade, a week to tune and prove, a week to ship and watch. The training itself is a few hours on the box.

Does our writing leave the building to train?

No. Examples are pulled onto the box, deduplicated and hashed there, and the adapter trains on the same hardware. The gate's sources never leave either.

Local or API

What runs on the box?

An open model (Qwen 3.8 is our current pick), your adapter, the index over your source files, and the gate. Your documents are indexed on that box; questions and answers never leave the building, and there is no per-token bill.

When would we use a frontier model?

When a task genuinely needs the top of the reasoning scale. Those requests go out through our guard — screened for prompt injection, personal details hashed locally before the request leaves — and come back through the same gate as everything else.

Who picks the model?

Evidence does. Before your writing touches anything, we run the field against your real letters and show you the scorecard; it is on the models page.

Pricing and process

How is it priced?

A pilot is a fixed fee for one kind of writing over four weeks, hardware included. After that it is a flat monthly support fee per box, with no usage metering. Guarded API access is billed at the provider's token price plus our guard, itemised.

What do we have to provide?

One owner who can say what a good letter looks like, two or three people to grade for about three hours in week two, and access to the systems where your writing and your source files live.

What happens overnight?

People read the day's drafts and mark the misses. Those become the next adapter's training examples and, where the miss was a claim, a new gate rule. A short report is in your inbox each morning.

Can we leave?

The box is yours, the model is open, and the adapter, index, source list and logs are in standard formats. Stop paying support and everything keeps working; you stop getting the overnight grading and the updates.
